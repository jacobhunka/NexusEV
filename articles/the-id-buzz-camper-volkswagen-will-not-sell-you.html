The ID. Buzz Camper Volkswagen Will Not Sell You

The Endless Adventure  ·  Jul 9, 2026

A 2025 Volkswagen ID. Buzz with a pop top, an induction cooktop and a swiveling front seat is not something you can order from a dealer. It is something a shop builds after you hand over the van. In a new video, The Endless Adventure collects a converted ID. Buzz from Peace Vans in Seattle and drives it past Olympia to a campsite on the Washington coast. The hosts have lived in vintage VW buses all over the world and, by their own account, broken down in every one of them. The trip becomes a running comparison between the bus people romanticize and the one that actually starts. The video reports roughly 330 horsepower, power to all four wheels, and a claimed range near 230 miles. In practice, they say, plan on something closer to 190 or 200. Peace Vans restores old VW buses and, the hosts say, converts them to electric drivetrains. This is what the shop builds when it starts with a new van instead.

What is new here is not the van. It is the conversion. Volkswagen sells the ID. Buzz in the United States as a passenger van with rows of seats, and the video is explicit that none of the camping hardware comes from the factory. Peace Vans adds it, and the hosts say the same shop will also swap gas and diesel drivetrains in older VW buses for electric ones. That puts this build slightly ahead of the American camper market, where most popular conversions still start with a gasoline chassis because range and charging shape where a camper can realistically go. Two practical questions the video never settles are worth asking any builder before signing. What does the conversion cost, and how does the work sit with Volkswagen's factory warranty? The hosts also mention that the campsite they use belongs to Peace Vans and is reserved for people renting from the shop, which suggests renting is the sensible way to test the idea before buying into it. Resale is the other open question. A converted van is a used van plus somebody's cabinetry, and that is a thinner market than a stock ID. Buzz.

The tour covers a lot of small engineering. According to The Endless Adventure, the camper's electrical gear does not run off the traction battery at all. The shop installs a separate power bank with a built-in inverter, which the hosts put at around 1,500 watts and say recharges while driving or on shore power. The induction cooktop is the only 120-volt appliance in the van, and the hosts say they monitored its draw on a phone app and reckoned they had close to two hours of cooking at medium heat. Elsewhere there is a deep sink whose pump swings outside to work as a shower, a fridge with a lift-out bucket, and rear drawers the hosts estimate are rated for several hundred pounds. The pop top converts to a second bed by pulling a panel down. The trip also produced a lesson. The video says a charging stop left capped at 80 percent racked up about $14 in idle fees against $1.29 of electricity. The hosts say the car stopped at its preset 80 percent limit without prompting them, and the meter kept running. The tour also covers a swiveling captain's chair, hidden ambient lighting the hosts say looks factory-fitted, a table stowed behind a panel, and a rear bed formed by sliding both seats flat. Outside, they say the van sits an inch or so higher than stock and wears decorative vents that mimic the air-cooling intakes of the original bus.

Bottom line: this is the version of the VW bus dream that survives contact with a Tuesday. The nostalgia is intact, the breakdowns are not, and the cabinetry looks better than most factory work. Just be honest about what you are buying. Something near 200 real miles with a loaded camper is a weekend radius, not a cross-country one, and the fact that the kitchen needs its own battery tells you how carefully the whole thing had to be engineered around the car. Rent one first. If a short leash still does not bother you on day three, go spend the money.
