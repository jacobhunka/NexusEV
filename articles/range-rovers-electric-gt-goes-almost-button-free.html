Range Rover's Electric GT Goes Almost Button Free

Electrifying  ·  Jul 29, 2026

Land Rover is adding a fifth model to the Range Rover family and it is going to be electric. On the latest Kilowatt Half Hour, the Electrifying team describe a briefing where they were shown the interior of the car, referred to as an electric GT, with the exterior still under embargo. The description that stands out is how little is in there. The host says the cabin is stripped back to almost nothing, with no physical controls beyond door switches and legally required items like the hazard light, which he compares to a Polestar and Tesla hybrid rather than anything Land Rover has built before. It is a notable direction to take at a moment when much of the industry has been quietly reinstating knobs. The episode also runs through a lease deal on a BYD Seal at £332 a month, a used Kona listed under £10,000, and a longer argument about whether efficiency now matters more to buyers than headline range.

The car sits on JLR's EMA architecture. The host says EMA was originally intended to be electric only and is now described as electric first, meaning it can also take a combustion engine. That shift is not unique to JLR. Several manufacturers that committed to dedicated electric architectures have since adapted them to take engines as demand growth slowed, so the change of wording is a familiar one. For a buyer, the practical consequence is worth understanding: a platform designed around a battery and later adapted, or designed to take both, usually involves compromises in floor height, tunnel space or frunk volume that a battery only design avoids. The team also relay that the term used at the briefing was white space product, the same one applied to the Evoque, and note the car is meant to be the most car like Range Rover so far.

The host says he put the button question directly to a company representative and was told the car will launch in this configuration, but that whether it stays that way is not settled. He notes that is an unusual thing for a manufacturer to volunteer before launch. His co-host adds that she saw the same car separately over the Goodwood Festival of Speed weekend, but only under a wrap in a darkened room, which gives some sense of how tightly the exterior is still being held. The episode's other useful thread comes from a listener, Julian, who wrote in about servicing. He reports a main dealer quoting over £400 for a first service on a Volkswagen ID.4, a second agent quoting £270 plus a pollen filter and a brake fluid change on a two year old car with 20,000 miles, and £55 to replace a key fob battery. One host says he was quoted £340 for a two year service on his ID. Buzz to maintain the warranty. Worth knowing if you are in the same position: under UK block exemption rules, servicing at a qualified independent garage to the manufacturer's schedule does not void a new car warranty. For contrast, the team say a first year service on a Renault 5 was quoted at £200, the same as on a petrol Clio.

Bottom line: the servicing complaint is the more important story of the two. Brake fluid intervals are set by the schedule, not the powertrain, so an EV still gets one, but £55 for a key fob battery is the kind of line item that turns a happy owner into an angry one. The industry's answer, that dealers rely on servicing because margins on the cars themselves are thin, is an explanation rather than a defence. Get an independent quote before your first service. As for the Range Rover, a button free cabin the maker will not commit to keeping is not a confident start.
