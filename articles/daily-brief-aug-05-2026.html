Ask the Owner First, and Everything Else Gets Easier

nexusEVnews Daily Brief  ·  Aug 5, 2026

There is a version of the electric car story told entirely in numbers, and today it mostly did not hold up. What held up instead was a smaller, less glamorous idea: the products that came off best were the ones where somebody had clearly thought about the person who has to live with the thing.

The clearest example is a charging site in rural Kentucky. PowerUp America's first location has a canopy, pull through stalls, tap to pay and both connectors on every port, and the founders' explanation is simply that they drive electric cars themselves and got tired of standing in the weather fumbling with an app. None of that shows up on a spec sheet. All of it is the difference between a charging stop you tolerate and one you route to on purpose. The same instinct shows up in Toyota's bZ Woodland, where the features Doug DeMuro likes most are a household power outlet in the cargo area, seat release latches so you do not walk around the car, and a console that opens from either side. It also shows up in what is missing, since the same car has no glove box and no front trunk.

Run the pattern in reverse and you get an Ioniq 5 drive motor replacement that requires dropping the entire rear subframe because, as the shop shows, two brackets sit about an inch and a half into the motor's path. Nobody decided to make that repair hard. Nobody decided to make it easy either, and at 153,000 miles the owner pays for that omission. Meanwhile the Denza Z spends its most interesting minutes not on horsepower but on parking itself into a bay narrowed by a concrete pillar, which is a problem actual owners have.

And then there is measurement, which is the same argument one level up. The InsideEVs podcast spends its best segment on why autonomous crash data and human crash data cannot be compared cleanly, and lands in the same place on EPA range: the numbers exist, they are collected honestly, and they were not designed around the question a buyer is actually asking. Watch for that to become the fight of the next six months. Standardized AV reporting and a highway range figure are the same reform wearing different clothes.

Bottom line: Horsepower is solved, range is close enough, and charging speed stopped being interesting a while ago. The companies that win the next five years are the ones treating ownership as the product: serviceable hardware, a charging stop with a roof on it, and numbers that answer the question people are asking. The ones optimizing for the spec sheet are competing for a scoreboard nobody reads anymore.
