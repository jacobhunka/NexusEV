Solar Grew Faster Than Any Other Power Source in 2025, Reports Say

CBC News  ·  Jul 7, 2026

Two reports out this week make the same point from different angles: solar is now growing faster than any other power source. CBC News, reporting on studies from the International Energy Agency and the UK think tank Ember, says solar's rise from 2024 into 2025 outpaced every other fuel, and that the gains are being led by big emitters like China and India. One figure the segment highlights is that global solar output is now large enough to cover the entire electricity demand of the European Union, meaning all 27 member countries. The reports also conclude that renewables can now meet the world's year-over-year growth in electricity demand, so there is no need to start new fossil fuel projects just to keep the lights on. The CBC correspondent calls them sunny stats, and the framing throughout is that the two big polluters driving the growth are also becoming the biggest solar players, adding capacity fast enough that they did not need to burn more fossil fuel to meet rising electricity use.

The timing is what gives the reports weight. CBC frames the story against nearly two months of tension around the Strait of Hormuz, a reminder of how exposed oil and gas importers are to supply shocks they cannot control. For readers weighing an EV, this is the part of the energy story that rarely makes the car reviews: a grid that leans more on cheap, domestic solar strengthens the long-run running-cost case for driving on electricity rather than fuel that has to be shipped in. The IEA and Ember are among the most widely cited bodies in energy analysis, which is why two aligned reports in one week drew attention. The near-term question is whether grids can build storage and transmission fast enough to use all the solar being added. The point the segment keeps returning to is that this is now as much an economic and security story as an environmental one, which tends to move governments that climate arguments alone have not.

The segment features Jessica Isaacs of the World Resources Institute, who argues that dependence on fuel imports is a constant drag on a country's finances, a cost it cannot control that plays an outsized role in its economy, and that switching to renewable electricity can insulate an economy from those supply shocks. She points beyond the usual examples of Norway and Denmark to Pakistan, where the report says rapid solar deployment has helped, with the World Resources Institute estimating roughly 12 billion in costs avoided during the current oil crisis. The reporter notes Pakistan still depends on Iranian oil and gas, so the shift is a buffer rather than a full break, and recalls the country's earlier deadly floods, which the segment says were supercharged by climate change. The reporter is also careful to say experts do not see this as an all-or-nothing switch; the point made is that moving away from imported fuel is becoming the economically prudent choice, not a purely environmental one. Claims and figures are presented as reported by CBC and the two studies.

Bottom line: The stat that should stick is that renewables covered all the new demand, because it reframes the debate. The argument is no longer only about climate; it is about which countries want their power bills set by a fuel market they cannot control. For EV owners, a cheaper and cleaner grid quietly improves the math on every mile. The honest caveat is that adding panels is the easy part. Storage, transmission and grid upgrades are the slow, unglamorous work that decides whether these numbers keep climbing, and they are where the next headlines will be won or lost.
