Why EV Battery Recycling Does Not Always Pay, and How Colorado Is Responding

NPR  ·  Jul 14, 2026

Recycling an electric vehicle battery is supposed to be the responsible, even profitable, end of an EV's life. NPR reports that the reality is often the opposite: salvage yards are getting stuck with old batteries nobody wants, sometimes having to pay just to get rid of them. The piece opens at Everett Auto Parts in Massachusetts, where co-owner Thomas Andrade ships out two Chevy Volt hybrid batteries and is happy to make nothing on the deal, because at least the recycler will take them at no cost. Across the state, NPR says, a salvage yard has a working Tesla battery it cannot sell, and the only recycling quote it received was negative $1,800, meaning the yard would have to pay to dispose of it. The economics, NPR reports, are not penciling out.

This matters well beyond one scrapyard. NPR explains that EV batteries treated as trash become hazardous waste, with risks of toxic leaching and fires, and that recycling their minerals eases United States reliance on China for critical materials. So there is a public interest in getting this right, even when the private math fails. The report ties the problem partly to chemistry. Manufacturers are shifting toward lithium iron phosphate, or LFP, batteries, which the piece says last longer and cost less but hold little value to recyclers because they lack the pricey nickel and cobalt that make recycling profitable. That is a useful counterpoint to the usual EV story, where LFP is celebrated as the cheap, durable chemistry bringing prices down. NPR's reporting shows the same trait that helps buyers up front can create a disposal headache years later, a trade-off rarely mentioned at the point of sale. NPR adds a structural reason salvage yards struggle with EVs at all: an electric car has fewer of the valuable parts, like engines and transmissions, that make a gas car worth dismantling, which the Automotive Recyclers Association describes as leaving the battery as the main wild card.

The numbers in the piece come from industry sources NPR quotes directly. An analyst at Benchmark Mineral Intelligence describes a gate fee, a charge to accept scrap, of roughly $1.50 to $2 per kilogram for LFP batteries in North America, which NPR notes can add up to hundreds of dollars per battery before shipping a hazardous material. Executives at large recyclers like Redwood Materials sound more optimistic in the report, and General Motors says recycling its factory scrap has become a source of revenue, but the view from small salvage yards is bleaker because they lack the volume to strike good deals. NPR reports the problem is expected to grow as today's EVs age and more batteries reach the end of their lives. The piece closes on a policy response: Colorado has passed a law placing responsibility for recycling unwanted EV batteries on the automakers that sold them, a producer-responsibility approach that industry groups and recyclers told NPR they support. The report also notes the batteries get shredded into a fine powder called black mass, from which minerals can be recovered, and it warns, citing a scientist with the Union of Concerned Scientists, that stranded batteries risk catching fire and are already turning up illegally in landfills. A state senator who co-sponsored the Colorado bill sums up the logic to NPR: the people making the thing being thrown away should have to plan for its end of life.

Bottom line: This is the part of the EV transition nobody puts in a keynote, and it deserves more attention than it gets. The uncomfortable truth in NPR's reporting is that a cleaner, cheaper battery can also be a near-worthless one at the scrapyard, and pretending recycling always pays for itself has left small operators holding the bag. Colorado's producer-responsibility law looks like the sensible fix: if a company profits from selling the battery, it should own the cost of retiring it. Whether other states follow, as the piece suggests they might, will decide whether these batteries become resources or roadside liabilities.
