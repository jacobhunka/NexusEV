The Self-Starter Test

A starter motor ended the electric car's easiest sales pitch in 1912, and today's videos test which EV advantages could vanish as quickly.

nexusEVnews  ·  Sep 11, 2026

Before the First World War, electric cars were sold on ease. Mike the Car Geek's history of the Detroit Electric shows it in the advertising: no crank to swing, no gears to shift, no half hour spent waiting for steam, and a charger at home for buyers wealthy enough to have electricity. Then, as the video tells it, Cadillac put a self-starting motor on a gasoline car in 1912, other makers followed quickly, and filling stations spread. The electric car's best selling point had become a part anyone could fit. By the 1920s, higher prices and less power left it behind.

That is a useful test for today's other stories. Is the advantage a component a rival can buy, or a structure it would have to rebuild? RJ Scaringe gave the clearest answer on the MOVE 2026 stage. At a traditional carmaker, he said, changing what a car does when its owner walks up can mean coordinating between 7 and 15 suppliers' control units. At Rivian, by his description, it is one software build the company controls. Volkswagen Group did not solve that by buying a part. By Scaringe's account it signed a $5.8 billion deal with Rivian in late 2024, and the teams have since adapted Rivian's system for everything from a small car he put at around 20,000 to premium Audis and Porsches.

Vehicle-to-grid passes the test differently. A parked petrol car cannot soak up cheap midday power and hand it back to the house at the evening peak. Yet the hold-ups Amber Electric describes are approvals, software and paperwork: networks that sign off at different speeds, young charger code, and carmakers that have not agreed to cover V2G under their battery warranties. BYD's 1,000 trial warranties may matter more than any charger. Out of Spec BITS argues Tesla's case from the software side. The host calls FSD 14.3.9 the most luxurious feature a car can have and says other systems trail it, while also arguing that a driver who is legally responsible should be allowed to set a maximum speed.

Slate's bet looks more like the Detroit Electric's: simplicity as the product. The video shows a small truck turning full circles and backing into a Chicago alley driveway, and says its color runs all the way through the panels. Simple is also the easiest thing for a bigger company to price against. Slate opened pre-orders in June at $24,950 before destination, and Ford announced its Fathom midsize electric pickup last month at $28,350.

The next few months will show which of these advantages hold once someone matches them. Scaringe says supervised point-to-point driving arrives by the end of this year, with Level 4 by 2028. The Out of Spec host thinks Tesla is close to teaching the car when to leave a drive-thru window. Amber's waitlist of more than 8,500 is real demand, and Amber says it wants broad warranty support from carmakers, not just BYD. And one of Slate's undecided reservation holders asks for a nudge when a showroom opens, which makes a place to sit in the truck the next thing Slate has to deliver.

Deep Dive

A 1925 Detroit Electric Still Runs on 84 Volts and a Five-Click Controller

Mike the Car Geek

Mike the Car Geek rides in a 1925 Detroit Electric owned by Bill, a collector with two of the cars, and uses the drive to tell the company's story. By the video's account, a Detroit carriage maker built the cars from 1907, kept going after a 1929 bankruptcy, and sold about 13,290 vehicles by 1939.

The car runs on 84 volts from fourteen 6 volt batteries, seven at each end. Bill explains that the five-position speed control runs the two banks in parallel for the first three steps and in series for the last two, which is where the extra push arrives. The video puts output at about 5 horsepower, top speed at 22 to 25 mph and range at up to 80 miles, with brakes on the rear wheels only and a voltmeter standing in for a fuel gauge. The video mentions a 1912 range record; published histories put that company-sponsored test at 211.3 miles on one charge. The cars were expensive. Hagerty has reported that one 1912 example sold new for $3,200, six times the price of a contemporary Model T.

Scaringe on R2: 40 Million Decisions, One Operating System and a $50,000 Target

MOVE: Mobility Re-Imagined

Rivian founder and CEO RJ Scaringe took the MOVE 2026 keynote stage a week after R2 customer deliveries began, as the interviewer noted. He gave the price range as $45,000 to $57,000 and, because few buyers take a base car, expects R2's average selling price to land around $50,000, against roughly $90,000 for R1. Launching three vehicles within three months strained Rivian the first time, he said, so R2 launched in a narrow configuration with R3 held back. Supply is now the hard part, he said, pointing to trade issues and competition for DRAM from AI data centers.

On autonomy, Scaringe described two in-house chips at 800 TOPS each, plus cameras, radar and a single lidar, with supervised point-to-point driving due by the end of this year and Level 4 by 2028. He said Illinois and a Georgia plant, backed by a $4.5 billion Department of Energy loan he says was recently restructured, will ultimately take capacity past half a million a year. He put Rivian's commercial vans on the road at a little under 50,000; Rivian's shareholder letter in February counted more than 30,000 with Amazon alone. He said R2 and R3 were designed for Europe and right-hand drive, without giving a date.

Charging

Amber's V2G Trial Lets a BYD Run the House at Peak and Sell the Rest

Everything Electric TECH

Everything Electric visits Amber Electric, which is scaling a 50-site vehicle-to-grid pilot co-funded by ARENA into what it calls Australia's largest residential V2G trial, with a waitlist it puts above 8,500. Amber lists the hold-ups: some networks approve sites faster than others, bidirectional charger software is still young, and not every carmaker endorses V2G under its battery warranty. BYD, Amber says, has provided 1,000 warranties for the trial. Amber says owners set how much of the battery it may use and a ready-by charge, with no minimum plug-in time. Joining means switching retailer to Amber, which charges a subscription and passes through wholesale prices.

By Amber's figures, about half of customer savings come from running the home off the car and half from selling to the grid. Martin, a trial participant with a BYD Atto 3, a pool and older solar panels, says his bill has fallen by up to 50 percent in some months and about a third in others. He sets a 20 percent floor and 70 percent by 8 a.m., and says he has noticed no battery change in five months. The video says a car pack is around five times a 13.5 kWh Tesla Powerwall and could run a four-person home for about three days. Ford makes a home-power claim of its own, saying a first-generation F-150 Lightning could power a house for up to six days.

Review

FSD 14.3.9 Handles the Drive-Thru Queue, but Not When to Leave the Window

Out of Spec BITS

Out of Spec BITS takes a launch edition Tesla Model Y L, which the host puts at around $60,000, on an hour-long round trip for coffee with his dog in the back. The car runs FSD 14.3.9, which the host says Tesla sent to a few early access cars and then pulled after his had downloaded it. The release notes he reads list a rewritten AI compiler, credited with 20 percent faster reactions, and smart summon raised to 8 mph.

He credits the car with carrying speed through two yellow lights instead of braking hard, though it sat in the left lane longer than he wanted. In the Starbucks drive-thru it stopped at the order point and the window on its own, then began pulling away after the first drink, before the rest of the order, and he had to brake. At 100 percent the car showed 319 miles on its larger wheels. Dirty Tesla's highway run in a Model Y L, which we covered on September 7, calculated roughly 370. The host calls FSD his first requirement in a daily driver and says Ford's BlueCruise is far behind. Ford said last month that every Fathom, its $28,350 midsize electric pickup, will be capable of running BlueCruise.

News

Slate Goes Looking for Reservation Holders Who Have Not Ordered Yet

Slate Auto

On a Chicago stop of its viewer-requested tour, Slate's own channel sets out to find fence-sitters, people with a reservation who have not yet pre-ordered, using names supplied by Slate agents. The first, Sid, says it is his first time with the truck in person. He turns full circles in an empty intersection, forward and in reverse, then backs into an alley driveway with a light post in the way, which proves no challenge. He asks Slate for a nudge when a showroom opens. The second stop is a coffee shop, where the truck hauls oversized bags and the conversation turns to wanting a small city truck that can take plywood and bumps without losing value. The video explains that pre-order holders are grouped into production cohorts and can move to a later window, and says the gray color runs through the body panels under any wrap. Slate opened pre-orders in June at $24,950 before destination, according to TechCrunch. We covered its 103 mile moving run on September 9.

Bottom line: Buy an EV for the advantages that are hard to copy, not the ones that are easy to advertise. Software a company fully controls is what Volkswagen chose to pay for, and it is the strongest argument for an R2 at $50,000. V2G is the most useful idea in today's set, but no Australian should join a program without the carmaker's warranty in writing. FSD is impressive, and by the host's account it still will not let the responsible driver set a top speed. Slate needs showrooms more than it needs another tour.
