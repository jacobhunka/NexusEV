Boring Is Winning

A hydrogen push that sold 14 vehicles in Europe, a ferry built for one crossing and a Polo praised as dull: the unexciting option keeps winning.

nexusEVnews  ·  Oct 26, 2026

Europe sold 14 hydrogen fuel-cell vehicles in the first half of 2026, according to SNE Research data cited by Just Have a Think. Not 14,000. Battery-electric buses took about 28 percent of European bus and coach sales over the same months, the video says, in line with the ICCT's count of new EU registrations. Energy analyst Michael Barnard explains to the channel why the headlines run the other way: a hydrogen trial is new, so it draws a burst of coverage, while an order for a hundred electric buses is routine and may get a single story. Batteries have become the boring option. Across today's edition, boring is what is winning.

Ferries show why. Bloomberg's look at battery ferries argues that they suit electrification because almost nothing about them is a surprise: the same short crossing, the same dock, a known window to recharge. Incat's China Zorrilla carries more than 40 MWh for a 90-minute run across the Río de la Plata, and one voice in the film puts the share of short-distance ferries that will go electric at 95 percent, while ocean-crossing ships never will. Tesla is chasing the same kind of certainty on land. In Munro Live's factory tour, its engineers describe a Cybercab assembled from modules that are each tested before they meet, for a vehicle they say should cover at least half a million miles in fleet service. A robotaxi's duty cycle, like a ferry's, can be planned, and planning is what makes the cost per mile work.

Buyers are reaching the same place by a different road. Electrifying chose Volkswagen's ID. Polo over Kia's EV2 and gave the reason without apology: it is well made, sensible and not much fun, a rational pick rather than an emotional one. The complaints in Micah Muzio's R2 review point the same way. He enjoys the haptic scroll wheels and the rear glass that drops into the tailgate, but what he misses is a plain button to lock the car and Apple CarPlay. Neither is new. Both are things drivers already know how to use.

What to watch: whether China Zorrilla holds its timetable once Buquebus finishes commissioning, and how Incat's three faster ferries for Denmark's Molslinjen, the first due in service in 2027 according to Ship & Bunker, cope with the extra power they need; how quickly Tesla's Cybercab line runs once deployments catch up with it; whether the EU revisits its AFIR rule requiring a public hydrogen station every 200 km on the core TEN-T road network by 2030; and whether Rivian tells buyers which R2s lost seatback ventilation.

Battery Tech

The World's Largest Battery Ship Was Built for One Short Crossing

Bloomberg Originals

Bloomberg's Primer series visits Incat in Tasmania, where chairman Robert Clifford's yard built Hull 096, now named China Zorrilla, for the ferry operator Buquebus. The video describes a 130-metre ship about 32 metres wide, with room for 2,100 passengers and 225 cars, a 25-knot operating speed and 5,016 lithium-ion batteries holding more than 40 MWh. Those batteries weigh about 250 tonnes, yet Incat claims the full electric system weighs less than a fuel-powered equivalent with its tanks loaded. The limit, according to Incat, is power: the ship has roughly half what a conventional ferry would carry, and a Danish customer asking for 38 knots will need far more batteries. That customer is Molslinjen, which has ordered three 129-metre Incat ferries with battery systems of about 45 MWh each, Ship & Bunker reports. The ship left for South America in August aboard the heavy-lift vessel Black Marlin, and Interesting Engineering reports it was floated off in Uruguay in mid-September. Bloomberg puts total investment at $184 million, including financing from Banco Santander and the World Bank's International Finance Corporation, and says Uruguay put about $20 million into grid upgrades and ship charging at the port of Colonia. Charging is estimated at about 60 minutes per trip, the video says.

Review

Electrifying Picks the ID. Polo Over the Kia EV2 for Being Sensibly Dull

Electrifying

Electrifying's two presenters put Volkswagen's ID. Polo against Kia's EV2. The Polo on test, a 52 kWh Life Launch Edition, has a 211 hp motor and up to 281 miles of WLTP range, and the video says it reaches 62 mph nearly 2.5 seconds sooner than the Kia. The EV2 Air on test makes about 135 hp, takes 9.5 seconds and is rated at up to 275 miles; Kia lists that long-range battery at 61.0 kWh. Electrifying quotes DC peaks of 105 kW for the Polo and 115 kW for the Kia, both good for 10 to 80 percent in under 30 minutes. Vehicle-to-load is standard on every Polo but limited to the top EV2 trim, the presenters say, and only the Polo has ISOFIX on the front passenger seat. Its 441-litre boot is 79 litres bigger than the EV2's and larger than the 385 litres of the ID.3 Neo in our October 24 edition. The EV2 is slightly easier to climb into and comes well equipped, with heated seats and steering wheel on the entry trim. Before grants, the video lists the Polo at £28,830 and the EV2 at £28,000, but the EV2 Air gets the full £3,750 Electric Car Grant against the Polo's £1,500, which Kia says brings it to £24,245. Both presenters still chose the Polo.

Deep Dive

Inside the Cybercab Line, Where Every Module Is Tested Before the Car Exists

Munro Live

Munro Live toured Tesla's Cybercab production with Lars Moravy, the company's vice president of vehicle engineering, and his team. Their case for the unboxed process is that a car is a box: build the front, sides, floor and rear as separate modules, test each one, then join them. The team says final assembly needs only 25 fully automated stations, the line is about 78 percent automated overall, and floor space per car is roughly halved. The front module holds about half the car's parts, a 48-volt low-voltage battery and an inverter that can be reached without lifting the car. Brakes are by wire, a first for Tesla, so front and rear can be tested apart. The front and rear modules skip e-coat, the paint shop is gone, and the colored panels come out of reaction injection molding. Tesla's battery director grades the Model Y structural pack a D for how hard it was to build and what it cost. The new pack, with a steel enclosure and potting done module by module so a failed module can be swapped, gets an A minus. The engineers cite an 11:1 gear ratio, against 9:1 in most Teslas, and expect the car to run at least half a million miles. Moravy told Munro last year the pack would likely be under 50 kWh, Carscoops reported. Today, the team says, the line runs faster than Tesla can deploy cars and does not operate every day.

News

Just Have a Think Weighs the Hydrogen Truck Revival Against the Sales Data

Just Have a Think

Just Have a Think opens with a run of hydrogen announcements: JCB's production hydrogen backhoe loader, Volvo testing hydrogen-combustion trucks on public roads, Mercedes-Benz preparing about 100 liquid-hydrogen fuel-cell trucks for customers, and Toyota's plan to join the Cellcentric fuel-cell venture of Daimler Truck and Volvo. Against that, the channel sets 14 fuel-cell vehicle sales in Europe in the first half of 2026. Its core argument is energy loss: splitting water, compressing or liquefying the gas and converting it back to motion each waste energy that a battery path avoids. Chemical engineer Paul Martin says the literature puts leaked hydrogen at 33 to 37 times the warming effect of carbon dioxide over 20 years, because it slows the breakdown of methane and other gases. Analyst Michael Barnard says 71 of the 183 hydrogen transport projects in his dataset are defunct or have dropped hydrogen. The video's case study is Aberdeen, which bought 25 hydrogen double-decker buses and has now decided to sell them, with at least some apparently headed for battery conversion. Electrive reported the buses had been off the road since late 2024 because of repair delays at the city's refuelling stations. The EU's AFIR regulation still requires a public hydrogen station every 200 km on the core TEN-T network and one in every urban node by 2030.

Review

A Family Week in the R2: Clever Features, a Few Missing Basics

Micah Muzio

Micah Muzio spent about a week with an R2 Performance and his family. He likes the cabin, which he says keeps the R1S's charm, down to real wood trim, and finds rear knee room generous over a flat floor, though the rear backrest does not recline. The 28.7 cubic foot cargo area has a 120-volt outlet and a 40/20/40 split, and the rear glass drops into the tailgate on the two upper trims. His complaints are about basics. The car locks and unlocks by proximity, with no simple push-button lock to confirm it, and he still misses Apple CarPlay, a gap covered in our October 17 edition. He calls the ride on 21-inch wheels firm even in its softest setting. Charging from 10 to 80 percent takes about 30 minutes through its NACS port, he says. Rivian told him seatback ventilation was streamlined in production because of cabin noise; owners place the change at around VIN 10,000, Drive Tesla Canada reports. Muzio recommends the base rear-drive Standard and expects a $45,000 version early in 2027. InsideEVs reports Rivian's schedule differently: a $48,490 Standard in the first half of 2027 and the $45,000 version late that year, while the Performance launched at $57,990 with its Launch Package.

Bottom line: Spend on the dull thing that works. In Britain that is the ID. Polo, though at £24,245 after its grant the EV2 Air is the better value if the Polo's bigger boot and quicker motor do not matter to you. In America, wait for the R2 Standard rather than paying $9,500 more for a Performance whose speed you will rarely use. And treat each new hydrogen road-transport announcement as a press event until European sales reach three digits. The technology that has stopped making headlines is the one already doing the work.
