The Stated Number and the Measured One Are Drifting Apart

nexusEVnews Daily Brief  ·  Jul 27, 2026

A widening gap runs through today's five stories: the distance between what a number claims and what it turns out to be worth. Not a story about lying. A story about how many figures in this market now carry conditions the figure itself does not show you.

Start with the cheapest car of the day. Rich Rebuilds paid $4,100 for a Lucid Air that will not boot, and the listings he shows put running cars from the same line at $6,500 and $8,000. Those prices are not a reading of the cars. As we cover in the salvage Lucid story, the host attributes the collapse to panic around the brand rather than anything found underneath the vehicles, and what he finds under his own car is an intact battery shield and unfired airbags. The price knew about sentiment. It knew very little about the car.

The same gap shows up in more respectable places. Electrifying's used Q4 e-tron guide spends most of its length explaining that the badge on the boot lid, 35 or 40 or 45 or 55, tells you less about the car you are buying than the option packs somebody ticked three years ago. Car Question's Subaru Uncharted review lands in the same place from the other direction: the badge says Subaru, the platform says Toyota, and one version does not even come with the all wheel drive that made the badge mean anything. And Out of Spec's side by side efficiency test found the R2 using roughly 18 to 26 percent more energy than a Model Y at every speed tested, after suggesting the two cars' EPA figures may not have come from the same procedure in the first place.

Even the racing fits. In Tokyo, the car that started from pole did not win, and the driver who leaves Japan leading the championship finished third from a grid slot in the teens.

What to watch over the next six months is who takes over the checking. Independent testers run side by side comparisons regulators do not, the Audi guide tells UK buyers to seek out dealers who will produce a verifiable battery health score, and salvage buyers price brand risk with no data. That work is unpaid and inconsistent. It is also becoming the only place a buyer gets a number with its conditions attached.

Bottom line: manufacturers are losing control of their own numbers, and mostly to people with a tire gauge and an afternoon. Rivian's efficiency claim survived exactly as long as it took one channel to borrow two cars. The brands that adapt will start publishing the conditions alongside the figure, because someone is going to find them anyway. The ones that keep quoting the best case will keep getting audited in public.
