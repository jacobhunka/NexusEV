Every Omission Was a Decision

Two electric SUVs land 100 miles and 200 kilowatts apart, and only one of them explains what it gave up to get there.

nexusEVnews  ·  Aug 14, 2026

Put two numbers next to each other. Throttle House says the BMW iX3 peaks at 400 kW and covers 698 km. CarGurus UK says the Alpine A390 GTS peaks at 190 kW and, on the wheels it actually comes with, manages 311 miles. On a comparison table that is a rout. Yet the Alpine review is the more admiring of the two, and the reason is that Alpine can tell you exactly what it spent that range on.

The trade is legible all the way down. An 89 kWh pack instead of something larger, which the reviewer says brings the car in at 2.1 tonnes, in some cases half a tonne under its rivals. Cabin switchgear lifted straight from the Renault parts bin, which Alpine reportedly justifies as money saved here so it could be spent elsewhere. No frunk, because the bonnet line was drawn for looks. You can disagree with every one of those calls and still see that somebody made them on purpose.

Compare that with the omissions the iX3 does not account for. Throttle House notes there are no ventilated seats on a car with heated and massaging ones, on a hot day, at this price, and calls it a sign of a rushed launch. The climate controls live in the screen, and the hosts say plainly that a screen is cheaper than engineering a physical switch. Neither absence is a trade the buyer was offered. They are just gone, and one host spends much of the review turning things off to get back to the car underneath.

The Freelander 8 is the interesting middle case, because its omissions are declared on the badge. JLR design, Chery engineering, Huawei driver assistance, no BEV version at launch, and a boot Elliot Richards struggles to fit one small suitcase into on a five-metre car. Nobody is pretending this vehicle was built by one company. Buying it means accepting a specific set of things somebody else does better.

The other two stories show what happens at the ends of the spectrum. The Pod Point Solo 3S asks the owner to give up one thing, the decision about when to charge, and hands back cheap rates and a cash rebate for it. That is an omission with a price on it, which is the honest kind. The Berlin solar boat builders have the opposite problem: the thing missing is charging infrastructure that nobody is going to supply, so one of them is building floating solar stations and renting the rooms above them.

What to watch for. The useful question when a feature is absent is not whether it matters to you but whether the company will name what it bought with the money. Alpine names it. Freelander names it. Pod Point puts a number on it, up to £100 a year. When a maker cannot answer, the absence usually was not a trade at all, just a line item that lost an argument in a meeting you were not in.

Review

BMW's Cheapest Neue Klasse EV Is Very Good and Very Busy

Throttle House

Throttle House frames the iX3 as the smaller, cheaper answer to the iX, built on a dedicated EV platform rather than adapted from the combustion X3. The numbers they quote are strong: 400 kW peak charging, 698 km of range, 4.5 seconds to 60 mph, and a Tesla Supercharger session taking the pack from 10 to 80 percent in around ten minutes. The host who took it on a 500 km cottage run reports no range anxiety with the air conditioning going. He also says it undercuts the petrol X3 M50 despite the new platform, and that the interior materials are the better of the two.

The friction is everything layered on top. His co-host describes fighting the driver assistance in city traffic, being scolded for not checking a blind spot, and hunting for climate controls in menus. Both single out the door handles and the steering wheel graphics. The omission that stings is ventilated seats, absent on a car offering heating and massage. Rivals are not standing still here either: the A390 GTS in story two keeps physical climate switches, and its reviewer names that as a reason to prefer its cabin. Throttle House says the iX3 is already selling heavily regardless.

Review

Alpine Traded Range for Weight and CarGurus Thinks It Worked

CarGurus UK

At £69,390, about £8,000 above the standard GT, the A390 GTS gets 470 horsepower, over 600 lb ft, and a 0 to 62 mph time just under four seconds, according to CarGurus UK. Three motors, two of them on the rear axle, do the torque vectoring. There are no adaptive dampers and no air suspension, just hydraulic bump stops, and the reviewer rates the ride highly anyway on the standard 21 inch wheels.

The costs are unusually clear. The 89 kWh pack is smaller than several rivals carry, which is how the car lands at 2.1 tonnes. Standard wheels drop the quoted range to 311 miles, and the 432 mile figure requires paying to downsize to the GT's 20 inch rim. Peak charging is a claimed 190 kW for a 10 to 80 percent stop of roughly 27 minutes, against the iX3's claimed 400 kW in story one. There is no front boot, though he measures the rear boot at 532 litres and says that beats the BMW's. The reviewer also flags the Volvo EX60 as a rival capable of nudging 500 miles, and says the middle rear seat is the short straw for families of five.

Charging

The Home Charger That Pays You to Stop Choosing When

Andrew Till / Mr. EV

Andrew Till follows a swap from an old Pod Point to a tethered Solo 3S. The film is built around Pod's own hardware and rewards scheme, so treat the figures as the company's. Pod, which the video says is owned by EDF and has installed over 300,000 chargers, prices the unit at £999 untethered or £1,049 tethered. A subscription runs £99 up front plus £34 a month over 36 months, roughly £274 more, but adds two years of warranty and a 48 hour repair guarantee.

The mechanism worth understanding is Pod Rewards. The owner sets a target percentage and a ready-by time, and the charger picks the hours, favouring cheap or renewable-heavy periods. Payouts are cash from £10, capped at around £100 a year, and the video says it works on any supplier and any single or two-rate tariff. Anyone on a supplier-managed smart EV tariff is excluded, since the scheduling happens there. Note the ceiling: this is a 7 kW unit, and the video says Pod does not make an 11 kW three-phase version. Five weeks in, the customer reports just under £12.

Solar

Berlin's Solar Liveaboards Are Building Their Own Marinas

SolarYacht

Two Berlin builders appear in a film produced around Victron Energy hardware, so read it as a supplier showcase. Eric builds aluminium catamarans and trimarans in Brandenburg and lives aboard one. His boat runs a 48 volt system with over 102 kWh of lithium iron phosphate storage, makes its own fresh water from the lake and handles wastewater on board. He says it stays off-grid from April to September, and that a roughly 100 km move of a solar charging station to Berlin ran on solar alone. On a full-battery day he has charged his car from the boat, around 20 to 25 kWh, which he puts at 150 km of driving.

Thorsten's problem is the one that decides whether any of this scales. He says the 24 hour moorings near him have no water and no electricity at all, so he is developing a floating solar charging lounge with a hotel room above it. His numbers are modest by road standards: zero to 100 percent in about five hours, or an overnight top-up at 2.5 kW for a 30 kWh boat. The video notes Germany and other European countries are drafting rules pushing boats toward renewables, which puts that gap on a clock.

Review

The New Freelander Is British Design and Chinese Everything Else

Everything Electric APAC

Elliot Richards drives the Freelander 8 in China and reports it is a separate brand from Land Rover, designed by the JLR team in the UK with a second studio in Shanghai, and that it will not be sold through Land Rover dealerships. It is 5.11 metres long on a 3.44 metre wheelbase. At launch it is a range extender only, pairing a 1.5 litre Chery turbo engine with a 60.3 kWh pack on an 800 volt architecture for a claimed 300 km of electric range, 550 kW and over 750 Nm.

What he rates highest is the software, which is Huawei's, with roof lidar and around 30 sensors. His argument is that the update burden then sits with Huawei rather than the carmaker, an advantage Western brands have mostly not arranged for themselves. He praises the suspension, the rear wheel steering, and the physical climate buttons that the iX3 deletes. The one real complaint is the boot, which he estimates at under 200 litres and calls disappointing on a car this size, though folding the seats releases over 1,000 litres. He says a new Freelander model is planned roughly every six months for the next four years.

Bottom line: Drive the Alpine first. Everything it lacks was swapped for handling and weight, and ten minutes will tell you whether that trade is yours. The iX3 will out-spec it on any table and outsell it several times over, and Throttle House is right that most of its irritations switch off. But a car you configure your way out of is a strange thing to pay a premium for. Pod Rewards is the sleeper: £100 a year is not life-changing, but it is a company naming its price for your flexibility rather than just taking it.
