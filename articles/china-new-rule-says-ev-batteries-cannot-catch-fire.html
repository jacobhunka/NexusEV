China's New Battery Rule Tells EVs They Are Not Allowed to Catch Fire

Ben Alexxander  ·  Jul 6, 2026

From the first of July, China put a new safety standard into force that, as Ben Alexxander frames it, effectively tells electric vehicle batteries they are no longer allowed to catch fire. The video identifies the rule as GB 38031, paired with a companion standard, both effective at the start of the month. Where the old 2020 rule only asked for a five-minute warning before a possible fire, the new one adds a two-hour observation window after thermal runaway begins during which the battery must not ignite. The host says manufacturers face heavy financial penalties if a battery fails, and describes a battery of destructive tests, from external fire and overcharge to a bottom impact test, that a pack has to survive without leaking, rupturing, or burning.

This lands in the largest EV market on earth, which is why makers cannot treat it as optional. The video says electrified vehicles made up 62.8 percent of new sales in China last month, so a no-ignition floor reshapes how nearly every pack sold there is engineered. For an outside anchor the video does not give: thermal runaway is the industry term for a battery producing more heat than it can shed, and Western safety work has leaned on delaying and containing it rather than banning ignition outright, which is what makes a two-hour standard notable. The host argues the push is less about a rising fire rate, which he says has been falling, and more about how hard these fires are to control and how fast footage of them spreads online, undermining trust in otherwise good cars.

The commercial response is already showing up. Alexxander reports that Chery has launched a Rhino battery guarantee across its Chery, Exceed, Jetour, and iCar brands, offering first private owners a lifetime warranty on the battery, motor, and control unit, and promising a replacement vehicle if a genuine battery fault causes a thermal runaway that damages or burns the car. He cites Chery figures of around 1.2 billion kilometers covered across roughly 43,000 vehicles with no battery accidents, while noting clearly that this is company-supplied data, not independently verified. The host also says CATL has expanded to around 170,000 staff, many of them engineers, as suppliers race to meet the tougher bar. He is careful to flag that the warranty carries the usual conditions.

The testing regime is where the rule shows its teeth. Alexxander describes seven tests at the cell level and seventeen at the pack level, spanning short circuits, overcharging, and external fire. He details a bottom-impact test in which a pack is struck three times by a small, roughly ten-kilo impactor, and a fast-charging clause requiring any battery that can reach 20 to 80 percent in under fifteen minutes to survive hundreds of back-to-back fast-charge cycles followed by a short-circuit test. Every car also needs a physical one-touch high-voltage disconnect, not a software button. On scale, the host cites around 1.37 million new energy vehicles retailed in China in June, and says China's fire rate had already been falling for years, though he stresses that figure is from memory. His point is that the rule targets how uncontrollable and viral these rare fires are, more than a rising trend.

Bottom line: This is the shift worth watching. When a government makes not-catching-fire a pass-or-fail test, battery safety stops being marketing and becomes a spec, and makers start competing on it the way they compete on range and price. Chery turning that into a lifetime guarantee is a smart read of the moment, even with the fine print. The open question for Western buyers is whether these standards migrate outward, because a two-hour no-ignition rule would be a genuinely high bar for any automaker to clear, wherever the car is sold.
