The Tesla Semi Faces Its First Fatal Crash Investigation

Kim Java  ·  Jul 10, 2026

A Tesla Semi was involved in a fatal crash on June 28 roughly 40 miles east of Reno, Nevada, and the hosts of this podcast, PJ and Kim, spend the opening segment laying out what is known and how little of it is settled. According to their reporting, the truck struck two vehicles stopped at a traffic light, two people in those smaller vehicles were killed, and a third person was airlifted to hospital. They say Nevada authorities closed the highway in both directions for hours and seized the truck for investigation. They also say statements at the scene indicated the semi's driver had fallen asleep. That account is preliminary, the investigation is ongoing, and no cause has been established by anyone.

The hosts are careful about the question everyone asked first, which is whether the truck was driving itself. They state that Tesla Semi does not have full self driving active on customer facing vehicles, and note it is not yet clear whether this particular truck was a customer unit or a Tesla one. What they cannot answer, and neither can anyone else right now, is whether any lower level driver assistance was engaged, including automatic emergency braking. That gap is where the story sits. It is also worth separating two things that get blended in coverage like this. Whether a specific truck performed as designed is a question for investigators, and those findings typically take weeks or months to surface in any detail. Whether heavy trucks in general should carry collision avoidance as standard equipment is a policy question that does not depend on the outcome of this crash at all.

The podcast places the crash inside a broader fatigue problem, reporting roughly 5,000 semi related fatalities a year on US highways and saying nearly all of the deaths are people in the smaller vehicles rather than the truck. The hosts argue, as an opinion, that this is precisely the case Tesla has made for automating a semi in the first place. The rest of the episode moves through other stories. They report Tesla pushing a fleetwide update that chimes and resists the door opening when cameras detect a bike or car approaching, and cite a figure that one in five bike accidents in major US cities involves a car door. They cover a $135 million California incentive program for first time EV buyers, applied at point of sale, capped at $50,000 for new cars and $25,000 used, and restricted to manufacturers headquartered in California, which they note excludes Tesla. They also play Rivian CEO RJ Scaringe declining, with a smile, to entertain a Tesla buyout, noting he has a duty to take any real offer to the board while making clear he thinks selling now would be a mistake. Elsewhere the episode covers Polestar being barred from the US market from the 2027 model year over connected vehicle rules aimed at Chinese linked technology, with existing 2025 and 2026 inventory still saleable, and CATL opening its two thousandth battery swap station in China with a stated goal of three thousand this year.

Bottom line: The instinct with a story like this is to reach for a verdict, and there is not one available yet. What can be said is that the trucking industry has known about driver fatigue for decades and has mostly managed it with paperwork. If the preliminary account holds, this crash is a fatigue crash that happened to involve the most technologically advanced truck on the road, which is the least comfortable version of the story for everyone. It does not indict the Semi. It does raise a question worth asking loudly: what is the argument, in 2026, for shipping any heavy truck without collision avoidance running by default?
