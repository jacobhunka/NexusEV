Nobody Buys the Peak Number

nexusEVnews Daily Brief  ·  Jul 9, 2026

The Zeekr 7GT Core is advertised at 450 kW. In Out of Spec Roaming's testing, it charges at 320. It also goes from 10 to 80 percent faster than Zeekr says it will. Both facts sit in the same charging test, and holding them together is the only honest way to read a modern spec sheet. The number on the poster and the number that shapes your afternoon have quietly stopped being the same thing. Today's five stories all turn on that gap.

Start at the bottom of the price range. The Fiat Topolino has 8 horsepower. Eight. Written on a spec sheet it is a punchline, and yet Car and Driver reports Fiat has priced it at $14,985. Nobody buying one is comparing torque figures. They are comparing it to walking, or to a golf cart. At the other end, the Rivian R2 makes over 500 horsepower, and the reviewer who drove it spent his time talking about legroom, car seats across the back row, and whether a charge port door feels flimsy. The horsepower was assumed. The door was not.

The same inversion runs through the rest. The converted ID. Buzz camper has 330 horsepower and four-wheel drive, and its owners spent the drive marveling that the cabin was quiet and the brakes worked. Its kitchen runs on a separate battery, because the impressive number in the floor turned out to be the wrong tool for boiling pasta. And plug-in solar, the technology the UK is about to legalize, is defined entirely by a ceiling: 800 watts. The interesting engineering happens beneath the cap, in the batteries that soak up everything the cap will not let through.

What to watch over the next six months is which companies figure out that the brochure is now a liability. Advertised peaks nobody reaches, ranges that assume a windless day, horsepower in a car judged on its cupholders: each is a small withdrawal from an account EV buyers are watching closely. The manufacturers confident enough to publish the boring number, the one you will actually experience, will end up looking like the only honest people in the room.

Bottom line: the spec sheet is losing. A car that beats its own charging claim is worth more than one that misses a bigger claim, and buyers are catching on faster than marketing departments. The winners here are the companies with nothing to hide behind: Fiat, selling 8 horsepower without apology, and Zeekr, whose battery is better than the number it is being sold on. The loser is anyone still writing brochures for a spec sheet nobody reads to the end.
