Three EVs, Three Price Tiers, and What the Extra Money Buys

Gjeebs  ·  Jul 15, 2026

Nobody cross-shops a $28,000 hatchback against a $76,000 three-row SUV, which is exactly why lining them up is useful. Gjeebs parks the 2027 Chevy Bolt, the Tesla Model Y Premium and the Hyundai Ioniq 9 Calligraphy next to each other and works through what changes as the price climbs. The Bolt opens at $28,000 in LS trim, and the RS in the video is optioned to roughly $35,000. The Model Y Premium, the video says, starts at $45,000. The Ioniq 9 Calligraphy on camera is a $76,000 car, though the video puts the model line's entry price around $58,000. What the walkthrough shows is that the money does not buy speed or range. It buys the parts of an EV you touch every day.

The framing worth keeping is that these are three different vehicles pretending to be three price tiers. A five-seat hatchback, a five-seat crossover and a six-seat SUV with captain's chairs solve different problems, so the interesting comparison is packaging, not equivalence. That matters for anyone shopping a budget rather than a body style, because the feature that separates these cars is increasingly software rather than hardware. All three, per the video, either use or accept the NACS connector, so the plug advantage that used to sort EVs into two camps is fading. What is left is route planning, preconditioning and how much configuring the driver has to do. Buyers should also note the running-cost angle the video does not raise: a 65 kWh pack and a 110 kWh pack cost very different amounts to fill, whatever the sticker says.

On specifics, the video reports the Bolt carries a 65 kWh pack and peaks at 150 kW, and argues the modest peak matters less on a small battery than the shape of the curve. It has no Apple CarPlay or Android Auto, running Google services instead, with route planning and battery preconditioning built in. Heated and cooled seats and a powered sunshade are available even low in the range, but there is no frunk and no powered trunk. The Model Y Premium, Gjeebs says, answers with power-folding rear seats, an 8-inch rear screen, a 15-inch front display on this car and 16 inches on newer builds, Sentry Mode and Full Self-Driving. The Ioniq 9 Calligraphy brings an 800-volt system that the video says charges at up to 350 kW and covers 10 to 80 percent in 24 minutes, an Alcantara headliner, second-row heated and cooled captain's chairs and CarPlay.

There are smaller notes that tell you more than the headline numbers. The Bolt, per the video, has no start button at all, coming alive when the driver puts a foot on the brake, and hides a large sub-trunk under the load floor holding the J1772 adapter. The Model Y answers with a frunk that has a drain plug, which the host suggests using as a cooler, and an in-car assistant that plots a multi-stop route with arrival state of charge at each stop. The Ioniq 9 runs a 110 kWh pack that the video says is the same size across the range, with the tested car rated at 311 miles and range shifting with wheel choice and drive mode. Gjeebs says he is considering trading his own Rivian R1S for one, mainly for the charging speed and the captain's chairs.

Bottom line: The Bolt is the honest one here. It gives up the frunk, the power trunk and the driver-assist crown, and keeps the two things that actually decide whether a road trip is pleasant: route planning and automatic preconditioning. If your budget tops out near $35,000, you are not settling. The Model Y still wins on software and charging reliability, and the Ioniq 9 is the one to buy if you carry people. But the story of this comparison is that $28,000 no longer means compromise. It means fewer buttons.
