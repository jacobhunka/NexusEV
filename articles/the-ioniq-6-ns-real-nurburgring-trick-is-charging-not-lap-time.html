The Ioniq 6 N's Real Nürburgring Trick Is Charging, Not Lap Time

Misha Charoudin  ·  July 26, 2026

Hyundai has a talking point for the Ioniq 6 N that has nothing to do with lap times. The video says the company calls it the 20/20/20 approach: two laps of the Nürburgring in roughly 20 minutes, about 20 minutes on a charger, then go again. Misha Charoudin put it on the clock during a track day. The car came off two full laps showing 22 percent, went on charge at 12:28, and 21 minutes later read 81 percent. That is the number that decides whether an electric car is any use at a circuit, and it is the one almost nobody publishes. The laps themselves were run in endurance mode, which the video says holds back some of the car's 650 horsepower so that two full laps are possible in the first place.

Track capable and track usable are different claims. Fast electric cars have been able to set a single hot lap for years. What they have historically struggled with is the second and third session, once the pack is warm and the software starts protecting it, and the pit lane conversation becomes a wait rather than a top up. Measuring recovery time instead of lap time is the more useful benchmark for anyone who actually books track days, because a car that needs an hour between runs quietly costs you most of the day and most of the entry fee. There is a caveat buyers should hold on to. The car in this video is not showroom specification. Charoudin says it is running Trofeo RS semi slicks, bucket seats and a half cage, although he also confirms the brake pads and the suspension are factory parts. The chassis impressions transfer to a standard car. The grip figures do not.

The driving commentary is mostly about calibration rather than power. Charoudin repeatedly praises the brake pedal modulation and the ABS, saying he went onto the brakes while the car was airborne and it stayed composed, and that the pedal was still consistent on his second push lap. He runs the stability control in sport rather than fully off, and reports seeing around 1.5 G through corners, with a 1.7 G peak on an earlier run. The second lap is driven using N Shift, the simulated gearbox, which the video says has shorter ratios than the Ioniq 5 N and adds shift lights and simulated overrun noise. At one point he catches himself thinking he should not have downshifted, which is a strange sentence about an electric car and roughly the whole point of the feature.

On the car itself, Charoudin describes the 6 N as a continuation of the 5 N that shares its powertrain but differs in suspension tuning, wheelbase and geometry, and says the operation has been simplified, with sprint mode now engaging maximum power automatically instead of requiring a button press. He sums the car up in threes: a lap he recalls the magazine testers getting under eight minutes, though he is going from memory and quotes roughly 7 minutes 35, under eight minutes of charging to replace one lap's worth of energy, and a purchase price he puts under 80,000. He also says he would like to build one as a competition car.

Bottom line: the interesting claim here is not that an electric saloon can lap the Nordschleife quickly. It is that it can do it, sit for the length of a coffee, and do it again, which is the difference between a demonstration and a car you would actually drive to a track day and back. Hyundai has spent two generations arguing that an EV can be entertaining rather than merely fast, and this is the first version where the operating pattern looks normal rather than negotiated. Just remember the tyres and the seats in this test are not what the showroom hands you, so temper the grip numbers before you get excited.

Commentary on a third-party video. Figures and claims are as presented in the source and have not been independently verified. Spotted an error? Tell us and we will correct it.
