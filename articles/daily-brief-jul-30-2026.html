The Headline Number Is the Least Useful One

nexusEVnews Daily Brief  ·  Jul 30, 2026

Ferrari has more than a thousand horsepower to sell and spent real development money on ways to deliver less of it, deliberately. That is the thread running through today's five stories. In each of them the number printed at the top of the spec sheet turned out to be the least useful thing on the page.

The Luce is the clearest case, and it landed here twice. Auto Focus drove a camouflaged prototype months before the reveal, and Top Gear drove a pre-production car in April. Both spend most of their running time on the same feature: a system that chops torque delivery into five steps so the driver has something to lean on at corner entry. An electric motor will happily hand over everything at once. Ferrari decided everything at once is boring and built five artificial ceilings to climb through instead. The peak output figure is the part neither reviewer dwells on.

Out of Spec arrived at the same place from the opposite direction. Its side-by-side highway run put a Rivian R2 rated at 330 miles at 253 measured ones, while a Tesla Model Y rated at 306 covered 310. Same road, same hour, same 98 degree heat. The window stickers ranked those two cars in the wrong order by 57 miles, and the explanation is procedural rather than mechanical. Meanwhile Mercedes is leading the electric GLA launch with up to 408 miles and 168 miles added in ten minutes, figures nobody outside the company has yet tested on a road.

Then there is the sodium home battery, which gives up the headline number entirely. It is bigger and heavier than lithium for the same stored energy and currently costs about the same. Its whole case rests on a figure most buyers have never seen quoted: 13,000 cycles with 85 percent capacity remaining. Bolted to a garage wall, density is a specification that does not matter and longevity is the only one that does.

Watch whether the ratings themselves become the story over the next six months. Independent testing is getting more rigorous and more public, and once the gap between a certified figure and a measured one starts moving lease residuals, it stops being enthusiast trivia.

Bottom line: the manufacturers that come out of the next few years intact will be the ones whose numbers survive contact with an independent tester. Ferrari worked this out instinctively and stopped selling the figure in favour of the sensation, which is why the Luce reviews read better than its reveal did. Anyone still quoting a range number they cannot reproduce on a real motorway on a warm day is running a clock they do not control.
