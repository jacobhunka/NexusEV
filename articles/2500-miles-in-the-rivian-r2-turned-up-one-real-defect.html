2,500 Miles in the Rivian R2 Turned Up One Real Defect

RivianTrackr  ·  Jul 31, 2026

RivianTrackr host Jose drove his new R2 Performance Launch Edition home the long way, roughly 2,500 miles from Rivian's plant in Normal, Illinois down to Orlando, Florida, and used the trip to sort the good from the annoying. Most of the drive was highway, and he says the majority of it was done on Rivian's Universal Hands-Free system, which he calls close to flawless on the interstate. Noise, vibration and harshness impressed him too: he reports the wind noise that dogged his R1 vehicles is simply gone in the R2, and nothing rattled after the car was put through hard testing with another crew. After all those miles, he says the only genuine defect he found was mechanical, not electronic, and it is a small one.

That single defect is a plastic cover over the rear doors' manual door-release, which Jose says pops loose on both rear doors when a door is shut. It is worth putting in context. Electronic door handles like the R2's are required by regulation to have a mechanical backup, so the trim that keeps rattling loose sits right on top of a safety-critical release, which is why he plans to leave it fully accessible rather than tape it shut. For a shopper cross-shopping the R2 against a Tesla Model Y, the more useful signal is what did not go wrong: the host describes a solid, quiet body over thousands of miles, and reminds viewers a cheaper standard R2 is still to come. The running-cost gap he cites is also real-world useful, roughly 15 cents per kWh charging at home against 40 to 45 cents at a DC fast charger.

The software told a more mixed story. Jose praises RivianOS 2.0 as responsive but lists bugs he reported: chargers vanishing from the navigation even while the car was plugged in, a false airbag warning cleared by a reboot, a stuck garage-pairing screen, and an energy app that briefly failed to register that the car was moving. He says he restarted the vehicle roughly once a day and that most glitches cleared. His sharpest complaint is range estimation. On the final leg he says the car promised arrival with a 5 percent buffer, then watched that buffer evaporate as he neared home, and he pulled in at zero percent without the navigation ever suggesting an extra charging stop. He also flags a quirk several owners have noted: the steering wheel gently vibrates between 60 and 70 mph, but only with hands-free engaged, which he suspects is a software behaviour rather than a fault.

On the drive itself, Jose rates the R2 highly against his own Rivians. He says it feels more planted than his R1S, which he attributes to the shorter wheelbase, and praises the semi-active dampers in their soft setting. He runs 20-inch all-terrain tyres and acknowledges they cost some range compared with the more efficient 21-inch setup, a trade he says is worth it for ride and looks. He also credits Rivian's charging, calling the R2 the fastest 400-volt car he knows of right now, though that is his characterisation rather than a measured claim. On the broader wave of R2 owner complaints circulating online, he is careful: he does not dismiss them, but argues the intense attention on a hyped new model surfaces every fault, and that early issues on any new vehicle tend to get resolved over time.

Bottom line: One loose trim piece across 2,500 hard miles is a good result for a brand-new vehicle, and the quiet, planted body is the real story here. The software still needs polish, and range estimation that lets you coast home on zero is the bug I would want fixed first, not the door trim. If you want a three-row you can skip, the R2 looks like a genuinely better use of space than a Model Y. Just treat the early software as a work in progress, and wait for the cheaper standard version if you can.
