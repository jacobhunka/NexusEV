Inside the Zeekr 7 GT, a Loaded Electric Wagon Near €60,000

Out of Spec Roaming  ·  Jul 11, 2026

Out of Spec Roaming's tour of the Zeekr 7 GT Privilege all-wheel drive is a slow, thorough walk through a car aimed at buyers who want a fast estate without a German badge. The video reports 646 horsepower, a 0 to 100 km/h time of 3.3 seconds, and a rear-biased setup with a 421 horsepower permanent magnet motor at the back and a smaller induction motor up front. A 100 kWh battery gives a WLTP range the host lists at 558 kilometers, with 400 kW-plus fast charging and a 10 to 80 percent time of about 16 minutes. It rides on air suspension with adjustable damping. The host frames it as a proper long station wagon, roughly 4.8 to 4.9 meters, and spends the video cataloging what €60,000 or more actually buys.

Fast estates are a niche the European brands mostly own, through Audi, BMW, and Mercedes performance wagons, and almost all of them are expensive. A Zeekr at this price is a direct challenge to that corner of the market, offering the same practical shape with an electric drivetrain and a long features list. It helps to know the pedigree: the host points out that Zeekr sits in the same Geely family as Polestar, Volvo, and Lynk & Co, and says this car was designed in Sweden. The tradeoffs are worth flagging for a buyer. He notes the 100 kWh pack returns 558 kilometers WLTP, which he calls adequate rather than efficient, so the case rests on charging quickly rather than going especially far. Choosing the all-wheel-drive Privilege trim also costs practicality: the front motor shrinks the frunk to almost nothing, where the rear-drive versions keep real storage up there. And several comfort features he wanted, cooled and massaging rear seats among them, are reserved for other markets or offered by rival brands.

The bulk of the tour is features. The video reports a 22 kW onboard AC charger across all trims, an NMC battery on this Privilege model where cheaper trims use LFP, and generous rear legroom for the 5 foot 9 host sitting behind his own seat. There is a long list of software modes: dog mode, camp mode, a nap-focused wellness mode, and a car wash mode that locks the car down, all over-the-air updatable through a 5G modem. Up front, the host praises seats that cool, heat, and massage at once, a 23-speaker system with headrest speakers for calls, and unusually large mirrors his wife singled out. Outside, he highlights matrix LED headlights, orange brake calipers, and a square Michelin Pilot Sport EV tire setup. He is more critical elsewhere. He says the mandatory head-up display blocks navigation from the driver cluster, calls the route planner slow and unable to save preferred charging networks, and flags rear vents that turned out to be fake. Rounding out the cabin are 40/60 folding rear seats, wireless CarPlay and Android Auto, a 60 watt USB-C port up front, and a heated but not cooled rear bench, which he singles out as a miss for hot climates.

Bottom line: This is a lot of car for the money, and the tour makes a strong case that Zeekr sweats the details most buyers actually touch. The seats, the charging speed, and the space are the real selling points. What would give me pause is the software, since a slow route planner and settings that will not save are the kind of daily friction no spec sheet warns you about. If you want a fast, practical electric wagon and you can live with menus that need patience, the 7 GT belongs on the list. Just test the software before you sign.
