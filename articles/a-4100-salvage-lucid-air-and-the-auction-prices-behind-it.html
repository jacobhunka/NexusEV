A $4,100 Salvage Lucid Air, and the Auction Prices Behind It

Rich Rebuilds  ·  Jul 27, 2026

Rich Rebuilds has bought a Lucid Air that does not run, does not charge and will not roll. He paid $4,100 for it. That number is not the interesting part. The auction screenshots he pulls up before laying a hand on the car are: a 2024 Lucid Air listed as running and driving with roughly 6,000 miles that he says sold for $8,000, and a 2023 car with 45,000 miles that went for $6,500. Cars that left the factory as six figure luxury sedans are changing hands at salvage auctions for used commuter money. The video's title puts this particular car at $140,000 while the host opens by calling it a $115,000 vehicle, so treat the original sticker as approximate. What follows is not approximate at all.

The gap worth studying is not between the new price and the salvage price. It is between $4,100 and $6,500. By the video's own screenshots, a running and driving 2023 Air with 45,000 miles sold for about $2,400 more than the corpse sitting in this shop. For anyone tempted to copy the project, that is the entire calculation. The cheapest listing is only cheap if you can wake the car up, and the premium for one that already moves under its own power is smaller than most people assume. Rich Rebuilds ties the pricing to nerves around the brand, saying an online bankruptcy rumor set off panic and that Lucid is now pursuing the person who started it. We have not verified either claim, and a rumor is not a filing. What the auction sheets do suggest is that salvage EV values move with brand sentiment as much as with damage reports, which is a risk conventional salvage buyers have never really had to price.

Getting the car indoors took two days. According to the video, it arrived from Florida parked hard against the cab of the transporter with the rear wheels locked, and the external jump terminals turned out to sit at the rear rather than behind the front wheel where the host went looking first. Power woke the doors and windows once, then nothing. The charge port light stayed red, the main screen never held a boot, and with no software access there was no way to release the parking brake. The crew ended up sliding the rear wheels onto greased plastic trays, winching the car onto a utility trailer, splitting the transporter to reach it, and repeating a smaller version of the whole exercise in the rain the next day. The host does credit Lucid for one thing: every door has a mechanical release that works with the car completely dead.

Underneath is where the money is, and it reads better than expected. The host reports fork marks on the carbon fiber battery shield with no punctures and no visible intrusion, hardware on the pack that suggests it has never been removed since the factory, and airbags that never deployed. He also finds coolant residue running down the pack with no identified source, a broken axle, a control arm ball joint that has separated, a shredded tire and curb damage on all four wheels. Body panels are rough almost everywhere, though the rear bumper turns up bagged in the back seat in decent shape. His verdict on camera is that the car is worth the attempt.

Bottom line: the pack looks survivable and the airbags never fired, which is the best news a $4,100 salvage EV can hand you. None of that will decide the project. It gets decided on a phone call, when he asks Lucid to sell parts and diagnostic software to a shop that is not a franchised dealer. A car that cannot boot cannot release its own parking brake, and no amount of talent with a winch fixes that. If that door opens, this was a steal at four grand. If it stays shut, he owns the most advanced 5,000 pound paperweight in America.
