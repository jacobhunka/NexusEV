Utah Legalized Plug-In Solar. A Missing Safety Standard Is Holding It Back

KUTV 2 News  ·  Jul 3, 2026

Utah became the first state to pass a law making plug-in solar, sometimes called balcony solar, as easy to use as any household appliance, and KUTV 2 News found that almost nobody is using it yet. The report follows Eric O'Brien, who bought three panels for about 100 dollars each and an inverter for another 300, a full system just under 800 dollars, and plugs it into a standard outdoor outlet. The inverter converts the panels' direct current into the alternating current a house uses, and an app shows he has saved 78 dollars since February. The idea, championed by state Representative Ray Ward, is that people who rent or cannot afford rooftop solar could buy a small kit, plug it in, and shave their power bill without an electrician or permission from the utility. The catch is a safety standard that does not exist yet.

Plug-in solar is common in Germany, where the report says it has spread quickly, and the appeal is obvious for the sizable share of American households that rent. The systems are light enough to sit on a balcony or in a yard, they move with you, and the report notes people in condos or apartments can take a kit along when they move. Several other states have passed or are moving on similar laws, the piece says, but Utah acted first. The piece explains what is actually blocking wider US adoption: while individual panels and inverters can already be certified, there was no national safety standard for a complete plug-in system until Utah's law prompted one. Here is the context the video leaves implicit: the core safety worry with these kits is backfeeding a home circuit through an ordinary plug, which is exactly why a listing body's sign-off carries weight before hardware ships at scale. UL, the same lab that certifies toasters and televisions, has now written a standard, but it cannot certify products until a technical committee is formed to approve it.

According to the report, the standard as currently written still says a homeowner would have to hire an electrician to install a system, which Representative Ward says runs against the whole spirit of plug-in solar. Ken Boyce, a vice president of engineering at UL Solutions, tells KUTV the goal is rigorous requirements that prevent electrocutions, fires, and injuries before the UL mark goes on any product, and that a committee will review and approve the paper standard, though he would not give a timeline. The video also features Chorus Striker of the nonprofit Bright Saver, which provides low-cost systems to low-income households and wants the German model, buy it at a store, bring it home, plug it in, made legal for an ordinary person here. O'Brien, for his part, monitors output through the EcoFlow app and expects to recoup his roughly 800 dollars within a couple of years, which he frames as the point: real savings without taking on a loan. Striker, meanwhile, casts the whole effort as a response to rising power costs and says advocates are simply waiting on the standard to clear.

Bottom line: This is a rare case where the technology is ready and the paperwork is not. An 800-dollar kit that pays for itself in a couple of years is a genuinely compelling pitch for renters and anyone priced out of rooftop solar, and Utah deserves credit for going first. But a law that legalizes something no certified product can yet fill is only half a win. The thing to watch is whether UL's committee moves quickly, and whether the final standard keeps the electrician requirement, because that single line decides if plug-in solar stays niche or actually reaches store shelves.
