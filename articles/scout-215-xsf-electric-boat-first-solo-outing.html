A Cracked Engine Block, and an Electric Boat That Saved the Weekend

Out of Spec Reviews  ·  Jul 6, 2026

The plan for the Fourth of July was a lake full of friends and a gas ski boat. What Out of Spec got instead was a Mastercraft with two cracks in its engine block, water pouring out where combustion should have been. The channel says the boat had been winterized poorly, froze, and split, which left a crew from as far away as Beijing and Norway standing on a dock with no way onto the water. The rescue came from a dealer a couple of hours away in the Norfolk area that sells only electric boats and jet skis. They loaned out a Scout 215 XSF, a roughly 21 and a half foot center console fitted with a Flux Marine electric outboard, an 84 kilowatt hour pack, and a demo price the host pegs near 130,000 dollars. This is the story of towing it home and taking it out solo the next morning.

What separates an electric boat from an electric car is the water itself. Most gas boats on a freshwater lake run an open cooling loop that pulls lake water through the engine, which is exactly why a bad winterization can freeze and crack a block. The Scout runs a closed loop instead, so the host notes there is no winterizing and nothing to drain, and the same hull can move from salt to fresh without a rinse. That matters more than it sounds: the video points out a saltwater-rated version of a comparable gas boat can cost tens of thousands more. The catch is charging. There is no marina fuel dock in the equation, but there is no Level 2 at the private dock either, so the crew leaned on a portable DC fast charger through the boat's CCS port to keep it moving all week.

On the water, the first lesson was weight. Out of Spec reports the boat struggled to climb onto plane and was drinking energy at roughly 7 to 8 kilowatt hours per mile while topping out near 11 miles per hour, until the crew moved bodies off the back corner. With weight balanced and the trim dialed in, the host says efficiency improved to around 3.6 to 4 kilowatt hours per mile and speed settled into the low to mid 20s, with a brief peak the display showed around 155 horsepower. The Flux system feeds a Garmin screen with live range that changes with throttle, showing anywhere from the high 30s of miles wide open to over 90 if you crawl. The closed cooling loop keeps the electronics in check, and the host reports motor, inverter, and battery temperatures all staying comfortable even during wide-open runs. By the end he and the crew were talking about how much better the boat would be with a dual-motor setup and a bigger pack, since it dipped from a high state of charge into the low 80s after only a short session of hard driving.

The towing leg had its own subplot. The truck was a Silverado EV, which the host says charged at 48 amps rather than the 80 he had hoped for, forcing multiple DC fast-charge stops on the round trip toward Virginia Beach. He also reads off the payload sticker at 1,717 pounds and a towing figure he places at 12,500 pounds, and notes the boat trailer used surge-style hydraulic brakes rather than electric ones, the same self-actuating design found on many rental trailers, which can fight you when backing up. None of it was dramatic, but it is a reminder that an electric boat still travels behind a vehicle with its own range math to solve.

Bottom line: This is not a review that says buy one. It is a near-perfect argument for where electric boating fits right now: a private dock, a lake you cruise for a couple of hours, and enough patience to learn the trim. Priced around a gas boat's double, the Scout only makes sense if silence, no winterizing, and salt-to-fresh freedom are worth real money to you. For a family that boats a few hours at a time and can plug in at the slip, it is genuinely compelling. For anyone charging at a public ramp, wait.
