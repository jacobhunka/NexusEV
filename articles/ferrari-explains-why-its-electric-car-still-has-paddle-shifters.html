Ferrari Explains Why Its Electric Car Still Has Paddle Shifters

Ferrari  ·  July 23, 2026

Ferrari's fifth film on the Luce, its first electric car, is not about range or charging. It is about how a company whose actual product is sensation manufactures sensation without an engine. The episode lays out what Ferrari calls the five generators of driving thrills: acceleration in a straight line, lateral acceleration, braking, the gear change, and sound. Four of those survive electrification more or less intact. The fifth does not exist on an electric car at all, which is why the engineers spend so much of the running time explaining what they built to replace it. The episode is titled Hamilton and Leclerc driving the Ferrari Luce, and most of it is engineers and designers talking through their reasoning.

Simulated shifting in an electric car is not a Ferrari invention, and the comparison is worth making. Hyundai's Ioniq 5 N is the production example usually cited, an EV with a simulated gearbox and matched sound, and the idea has spread far enough that Throttle House, in a review we also covered today, notes the new Lexus ES offers a simulated shift mode in Europe that Canada does not get. What makes the Luce version worth watching is the argument behind it. The speakers in the film say the point is not nostalgia but information: the sound is meant to tell the driver what the motors are doing, and the paddles give a physical event to time a corner around. Whether a buyer at this level wants either is a fair question, and the film is candid that the internal debate took a while to settle. It is also worth reading this episode against what has already been shown publicly. When Electrifying filmed the Luce at its first UK appearance, coverage we ran earlier this month, the figures reported were 1,050 hp, a 122 kWh battery and a four door five seat body with design led by Jony Ive. This film adds none of that. It is entirely about feel.

On the mechanics, the film says the Luce combines regenerative braking with carbon ceramic discs so that braking works both mechanically and electrically, and that the paddles do not operate a gearbox but change torque delivery, letting a driver drop down entering a corner and pick straight back up. There are four motors, described as responding together to steering input. On sound, one speaker compares a combustion engine to an acoustic guitar with a soundbox where waves develop and resonate, and an electric motor to an electric guitar: a solid core that genuinely produces pressure waves but needs a pickup to be heard. Ferrari's position is that the sound in the car is extracted rather than invented. The film also claims the Luce disguises its size, that its dimensions and wheelbase mislead in person, and that it can move between quiet comfort and track behaviour on demand. The design argument runs alongside the engineering one. One speaker rejects what he calls arbitrary styling in favour of what he describes as the beauty of functionality, arguing the design and the engineering cannot be separated. Another says outright that weight is the enemy in a sports car and that going electric was the only way to get the proportions they wanted, which is a claim worth remembering when independent kerb weights appear. The film closes on Ferrari framing itself as a company that started racing in 1929 and does not intend to spend 2026 guarding its own history.

Bottom line: Ferrari is doing the honest version of a thing that usually sounds dishonest. Every EV that fakes a gearshift is open to the charge of pure theatre, and the only real defence is whether the fake thing carries true information. Paddles that change torque delivery and a sound taken from the motors rather than a sample library clear that bar, at least in principle. What still needs testing by someone outside Maranello is the claim that the car hides its weight and size, because that promise is what the whole thing rests on, and no brand film has ever failed to make a car feel light.
