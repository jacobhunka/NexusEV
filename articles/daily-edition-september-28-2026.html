The Distance Is in the Price

A Semi plant that stamps its own panels, lithium pumped from beneath German farmland, and a Geely priced in yuan at home and in pounds here.

nexusEVnews  ·  Sep 28, 2026

Tesla says it stamps 62 of the Semi's parts inside its Nevada truck plant, builds the axles and seats under the same roof, and designed a powder coat line so it would not need a paint shop. In southwestern Germany, Vulcan Energy is drilling more than 3,000 metres down so Europe can get lithium from its own ground. Both are big bets on making things closer to where they are used. Geely's Starray runs the other way. It reaches Britain at £29,990, and its reviewer found the same model listed in China at 95,800 yuan. Today's five stories are about distance, and about who pays for it.

The Starray gap is easier to read next to the Everything Electric interview. George, an EV sales analyst at Benchmark Mineral Intelligence, says Chinese automakers squeezed at home treat Europe as a place to earn better margins, with tariffs adding to the price in some markets. So the difference between the yuan and pound prices is about more than freight. It also explains why the Cars Uncovered verdict still lands on good value: the British price only has to beat European rivals, not Chinese ones.

The rest of the day is about shrinking the distance, and none of it is simple. BYD, George says, began building in Brazil in July 2025 and passed about 100,000 vehicles in roughly a year, with orders from Mexico and Argentina on the books. He describes European-owned cell makers struggling to reach economic volumes while Korean and Chinese firms, who have scaled factories before, move faster. Vulcan's project pushes the same idea upstream, and DW does not let it off lightly: a 2009 tremor linked to the site, and a researcher who says geothermal lithium's footprint can exceed that of existing sources, depending on the brine. Tesla's Semi plant is localization inside a single building, down to the high-voltage cabling it now makes itself.

Distance means something to drivers too. On CNET's Reno run, the first charger the car picked was closed to its brand and the second was crowded, and the fix was a newer station one exit further on. That is infrastructure filling in, the same shortening at human scale. It only pays off if the cars keep selling, and in the US, George says, sales were still down 24 percent year to date at the halfway point.

What to watch: whether Vulcan's commercial plants reach the 24,000 tons a year they are being built for, whether BYD Brazil finishes its move from kits to full production, and the US third-quarter numbers, which George expects to look worse against last year's rush to beat the tax credit deadline. At the Semi plant, staff said standard-range trucks are only now running as samples. How quickly that version follows the long-range truck is the next production milestone worth tracking.

Out of Spec Walks Tesla's Semi Line, From Stamped Panels to Automatic Cab Marriage

Out of Spec Reviews

This is the site's third look inside Tesla's Semi plant in Sparks, Nevada, after earlier tours from Top Gear Tech and the Core Memory Podcast, so here is what Out of Spec's walk-through adds. The stamping team says it runs 62 part numbers in house. Cabs, roofs and closures are powder coated rather than painted, and the coatings team says the line is about a third the size of an equivalent liquid paint line, with close to zero VOCs. Per the engineer at that station, the long-range truck carries three battery structures with about 1,000 spot welds each, more in total than the roughly 2,800 on the cab. The aluminum high roof weighs about 170 lb with over 700 welds, and staff tie it to a target of 1.7 kWh per mile for 500 miles of long-range driving. Axles, seats with motorized belts and high-voltage distribution are all built on site, and the axle team says the stator and drive inverter are shared with Cybertruck. Tesla calls its cab marriage station the first fully automatic one. The host's reservations: roof fairings on display that looked warped to him, and an open question about service coverage in remote areas.

Battery Tech

Vulcan Pulls Lithium From Hot German Brine and Sends the Water Back Down

DW Planet A

DW Planet A visits Vulcan Energy in southwestern Germany, where the company pumps brine from more than 3,000 metres down, uses its heat for district heating, and then extracts the lithium dissolved in it. Vulcan's Manfred Boeckmann puts the concentration at 180 to 200 mg per litre. A heat exchanger cools the brine from about 160°C, then at 70°C it runs through a filter-like material that holds the lithium, and the depleted brine is reinjected. Vulcan says the commercial plants it is building will make 24,000 tons a year of lithium hydroxide monohydrate, enough by its count for 500,000 EVs. The company claims about 300 litres of freshwater per ton, a production cost under $4,200 per ton, and net-zero emissions once its geothermal energy is counted. The video sets those claims against two cautions. Researcher Vanessa Schenker says the footprint of geothermal lithium varies widely with the brine and can exceed that of today's sources, and her work puts emissions at 5 to 46 tons of CO2 per ton of lithium carbonate equivalent. DW also reports that a magnitude 2.7 event in 2009 was officially linked to activity at the site it filmed; Vulcan says the technology has since improved. The company declined to tell DW which chemicals it uses. Glencore has signed deals to buy Vulcan's lithium, and its head of lithium says a 5 to 20 percent market share is possible if projects deliver what they claim.

July Brought 1.85 Million Plug-In Sales Worldwide, and the US Is Still Down

Everything Electric

On the Everything Electric podcast, George, an EV sales analyst at Benchmark Mineral Intelligence, unpacks July's global plug-in sales of 1.85 million, a figure that counts battery-electric cars and plug-in hybrids together. He says it is not an all-time monthly high, since months above 2 million tend to come late in the year, but it is a record for July, usually a quiet month. He names Europe as the main growth engine in 2026, helped by returning subsidy schemes, with higher fuel prices tied to the Iran conflict lifting interest in some markets. Chinese brands facing a tougher home market are exporting in record numbers; he puts BYD's 2025 share in Europe at about 7 percent. BYD's Brazil plant, he says, began production in July 2025, reached about 100,000 vehicles in roughly a year, and is moving from kit assembly to full production. The US is the weak spot. After the federal EV tax credit ended in September 2025, he says, sales were down about 33 percent at the end of the first quarter and 24 percent year to date after the second. Tesla is up for the year in the US, he says, and Rivian about 17 percent. The Geely in story 4 is one of the exports he describes.

Review

Geely's Starray Plug-In Hybrid Lands in Britain From £29,990

Cars Uncovered

Cars Uncovered tests Geely's Starray, an SUV the host compares in size to Volvo's XC60, sold in Britain as a plug-in hybrid that Geely calls a super hybrid, pairing a 1.5-litre petrol engine with an 18 or 29 kWh battery. Pricing runs from £29,990 to about £35,000, and the reviewer quotes around £335 a month on 0% finance with just under £2,000 down. Quoted electric-only range is 51 to 84 miles, and an 8-year warranty is included. The car never has to be plugged in: a charge mode lets the engine top up the battery, and the trip display showed 35.2 mpg in that mode, which the host calls good for the size. DC charging tops out at 60 kW. He praises cruise control that follows speed limits on its own and the camera views. His complaints are specific. Lane-change assist did not respond in his test, though he was unsure whether his trim includes it, one-pedal mode never brings the car to a full stop, road noise is higher than he would like, and, by his own admission with some exaggeration, the driver monitor cuts his podcast when he glances away. He also found the Starray listed in China at 95,800 yuan, a gap he calls alarming, though he still rates the UK car as strong value. Story 3 offers one explanation for that gap.

Charging

CNET's Bay Area to Reno Run in a Chevy Equinox EV, Detours Included

CNET

CNET drives a 2026 Chevrolet Equinox EV from the San Francisco Bay Area to Reno and back, a distance the host notes is longer than most Americans say they are comfortable driving electric. He says he barely planned, and credits years of charger growth and his own experience for that. According to the video, the front-wheel-drive version is rated at 319 miles, charges at up to 150 kW and starts around $35,000; the test car stickers at just over $50,000, mostly for comfort and styling upgrades plus GM's Super Cruise. His advice is practical. Use a planner such as A Better Route Planner or the car's own navigation, set up plug and charge, keep one or two network apps as backup, and carry a CCS to NACS adapter, which he says costs under $200 for a reputable one. The trip still hit snags. The first stop the navigation chose was a Tesla station not yet open to other brands, the second was a crowded Electrify America site, and a newer Tesla station one exit up solved it. Charging from 10 to 80 percent took about 35 minutes, and the whole stop about an hour. For scale, the Starray in story 4 tops out at 60 kW, while Tesla ran a 1.2 MW charging demo at the Semi event.

Bottom line: The Starray is the best argument in today's set for buying now, and also a clear picture of what Europe's homegrown suppliers are up against. A British buyer gets a well-equipped plug-in SUV for under £30,000 while Geely, by Benchmark's logic, earns a margin it could not get at home. Both sides win, which is exactly why that gap will last. Vulcan's lithium deserves the closest watching. If it scales without the seismic and emissions questions DW raises, it will matter more than any single car here.
