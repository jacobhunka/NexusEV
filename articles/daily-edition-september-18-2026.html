What the Options List Leaves Out

Two X3 owners, a 321 horsepower Golf, a Roadster event and a desert charging stop keep circling the same point: the details that decide ownership were never on the order form.

nexusEVnews  ·  Sep 18, 2026

Volkswagen can tell you precisely what it bought with the Golf GTI's fiftieth birthday budget. Hagerty's Henry Catchpole lists the receipt: 321 horsepower, springs 20 percent stiffer, five millimetres off the ride height, 25 kilos out of the kerb weight, two degrees of negative camber instead of 1.3, forged wheels at eight kilos each. He says it is now the fastest front-wheel-drive production car around the Nordschleife, three tenths clear of a Honda Civic Type R. Every item can be specified, ordered and timed with a stopwatch.

Not one of them describes what the other four stories today are actually about.

Autogefühl's Thomas and Lea have lived with an X3 plug-in hybrid for roughly half a year, and their report reads as a list of things no configurator asks about. A grille surround that came back from its first car wash marked in a way that would not wipe clean. Hard plastic where they want fabric. No laminated side glass at this price. A boom from the rear axle on poor surfaces. And, arriving only recently, a high-frequency noise from around the tailgate above 80 km/h that they say was not there at the start and that neither their dealer nor BMW has yet explained.

Gjeebs spent 2,000 miles in a different X3, petrol and rented, and independently landed on noise as the car's main flaw, though he blames the tyres rather than the glass. He spent the same trip regretting the M Sport suspension. Two reviewers, two fuels, two countries, and the advice converges: specify this car for comfort, not appearance. Autogefühl chose 19-inch wheels for ride quality and kerb clearance, and Gjeebs notes the same size in a square, non-staggered fit lets you rotate the tyres and helps efficiency. Neither conclusion is reachable on a test drive.

Travis Ketchum's case for Rivian's Joshua Tree outpost is built from the same unspecifiable material: an awning, a bathroom that stays open when the store is shut, a cool room with chairs in it. On a driving day of 12 to 14 hours, he argues, those decide the trip more than the gap between 53 cents a kWh and something cheaper down the road. At the far end sits the Tesla Roadster, all specification and no ownership: a reported price above a million dollars for the version with rocket thrusters, and footage of something rising inside a tent that a careful watcher says was mounted on a gantry.

The thing worth following is the whistle. A manufacturer answer on a small, intermittent, hard-to-reproduce noise is the closest thing to a public test of what a warranty is for, and Autogefühl has already asked the dealer and BMW directly. The GTI's lap time will be confirmed or picked apart within weeks. The Roadster needs an unveiling first, and then several years of owners, before anyone can say what it is like to have one.

Review

Six Months With the X3, and a Noise Nobody Can Name

Autogefühl

Autogefühl's Thomas and Lea have had their BMW X3 plug-in hybrid for about half a year, and this is their first long-term verdict. The failure they lead with is small and instructive. After Lea's first car wash, the grille surround was covered in marks that looked like water spots and would not come off. Thomas says that should not happen and points at production, but the part was replaced under warranty with an X5 loaner, and he treats the dealer's response as the part that actually matters. Their remaining complaints are about refinement. There is hard plastic inside the doors where they would prefer fabric, no acoustic side glass offered, and a boom from the rear axle on rough roads. The newest issue is unresolved: a high-frequency noise from the tailgate area that starts around 80 km/h, which Thomas says was not there at the start and does not intensify as speed rises. His two theories are the pressure ventilation vent or a tailgate no longer closing tightly, and he has asked both the dealer and BMW without an answer so far. On the powertrain they report 80 to 90 km of usable electric range from a battery Thomas puts at roughly 20 kWh, but had to dig into a menu to stop the car refusing to fire its engine under full throttle. The noise complaint is not specific to the plug-in: the petrol X3 in the next story drew it from a different reviewer, who blamed the tyres.

A 2,000 Mile Road Trip in a $31,000 X3

Gjeebs

Gjeebs rented a 2026 BMW X3, the base four-cylinder car with M Sport equipment, and drove it from Phoenix to Austin and back to find out what owning one would really involve. He reports 255 horsepower and an official highway figure of 33 mpg, against measured fill-ups of 31.8, 32 and 32.1 mpg on stretches posted at 75 and 80 mph. That is about 92 percent of the official number, which he notes is far better retention at speed than he gets from the electric cars he usually drives, and he says over 500 miles between stops is realistic. His criticisms are specific. He finds tyre noise loud while wind noise is not, the M Sport suspension firmer than he would pick for long drives, and the base stereo is weak enough that he would wait for a car with the upgraded system. The value argument is the reason for the video. He says clean-title examples with around 40,000 miles are listed near $31,000, against a new RAV4 he prices at $32,000 with no options and closer to $45,000 as dealers actually stock them, and he expects supply to build as cars sold from late 2024 come off lease through 2026 and into 2027. Set beside the plug-in owners above, the advice lines up regardless of engine: both reviewers ended up recommending the smaller wheels and the softer suspension.

Review

The Most Powerful Golf GTI Ever Wants £50,000

Hagerty

Volkswagen marked 50 years of the Golf GTI with the Edition 50, and Hagerty's Henry Catchpole rates it the best GTI yet. He gives it 321 horsepower and 310 pound-feet, roughly 60 up on a standard GTI and 25 up on a Clubsport, with Volkswagen claiming 0 to 62 mph in 5.3 seconds and independent runs he says have come in under five. Adjustable dampers with 15 settings come as standard, he notes. The version he argues for has the optional performance pack: five millimetres lower, springs 20 percent stiffer, 25 kilos lighter, two degrees of negative camber at the front against 1.3 on the Clubsport, and forged wheels at eight kilos apiece. That specification, he says, made it the fastest front-wheel-drive production car around the Nordschleife at 7 minutes 44.5 seconds, three tenths ahead of the Honda Civic Type R. Price is the catch. He puts it near £50,000 once you add the pack that makes it worth having, against £29,000 for a base 1.5 TSI Golf, and no manual gearbox is offered at all. Volkswagen also moved the GTI badge onto the rear-drive electric ID.3 this week, which we covered yesterday, so the petrol car's anniversary and the badge's next home arrived within days of each other.

News

Tesla Sets October 1 for the Roadster It Showed in 2017

Kim Java

Tesla has set October 1 for a Roadster launch event, and the rundown from Kim Java is mostly a record of how long the wait has been. The car was shown in 2017, and the hosts count teases for April, May, July and August of this year before this date landed. They say the event is in Waco, Texas, and that reservation holders have been invited. The location interests them because SpaceX develops rocket engines nearby in McGregor. They also walk through footage filmed there in April showing an object rising inside a tent, and they handle it carefully: someone who follows the site closely says it was on a gantry rather than flying under its own power. A report the hosts cite describes two versions, one road-legal and one with a SpaceX package that could cost over a million dollars. For anyone holding a 2017 referral award or a Founders Series deposit, there is still no confirmed price or delivery date to plan around.

Charging

Rivian's Joshua Tree Outpost Charges More and Offers More

Travis Ketchum

Travis Ketchum has put a couple of thousand miles on a Rivian R2 and used the Joshua Tree stop to make an argument about what charging really costs. He shows peak pricing of 53 cents per kWh for Rivian drivers and 62 cents for everyone else, as low as 37 cents off peak, and an idle fee of 50 cents a minute once a car sits for 10 minutes after finishing. The night before, he says, the Barstow site was full and two cars ended up splitting power. His case is that the premium buys more than electrons: the site has an awning for shade, a trailer-friendly pull-in stall, drinking water, bathrooms that stay open when the store is closed, and an air-conditioned room with chairs. On days that run 12 to 14 hours between driving and charging, he argues, having somewhere other than the car to sit changes the trip more than the price per kWh does. He also flags a detail worth knowing before buying: the R2 and 2026-on R1 use NACS, older Rivians use CCS, and an adapter bridges the two because the underlying protocol is the same.

Bottom line: If you are buying either X3, take both reviewers at their word: 19-inch wheels, and leave the sport suspension on the shelf. Of the two, the used petrol car near $31,000 is the better deal, as long as tyre noise does not bother you. The Edition 50 is for people who will actually use two degrees of camber, and at £50,000 with the pack it needs to be. Rivian's premium at Joshua Tree is worth paying. The Roadster is not a product yet, whatever rises inside the tent on October 1.
