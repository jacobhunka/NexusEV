An Electric Plane Crossed New Zealand's Cook Strait, and the Real Story Is the Cost

BETA Technologies  ·  Jun 12, 2026

A fully electric aircraft has been crossing one of the roughest stretches of water in the Southern Hemisphere, and the team behind it says these were the first electric flights over New Zealand's Cook Strait. BETA Technologies, the American company building the airplane, spent the past few weeks running the gap between the North and South Islands, with Air New Zealand watching closely. The strait looks short on a map. It is the narrowest point between the two islands. In the air it is a different proposition, sitting in the band of latitudes that sailors call the roaring forties, where wind funnels through and the seas pile up. Doing that crossing on batteries, in roughly 20 to 25 minutes, is the kind of quiet milestone that says more about the next decade than the flight itself does.

The number worth holding onto here is the comparison to the ferry. The Interislander service between Wellington and Picton takes about three hours, and BETA's pilots claim their aircraft used less energy crossing the entire strait than that ferry burns just getting off the dock. They put a one-way electric flight at around 15 US dollars at current rates. New Zealand is an unusually clean test case for this idea. The South Island leans on the North Island for freight and supply, the population is spread thin across long distances, and short hops between regional airfields are exactly where a battery aircraft thrives, because it never needs the long range that grounds bigger electric plans. Air New Zealand has signaled for years that it wants regional electric aircraft flying this decade, which is why a trial on a genuinely useful corridor lands harder than a one-off demonstration ever could. There is a freight angle too. The pilots point out that the South Island depends on the North Island for almost everything it consumes, and cheap, simple ways to move goods across the gap matter to a country built on long, thin supply lines. An aircraft that is close to free to run starts to look less like a novelty and more like infrastructure.

The footage is short on hard specifications and long on intent. The pilots describe taxiing from the western ramp at Wellington, climbing out, and turning toward the strait, with the crossing itself taking about 20 to 25 minutes against the ferry's three hours. Their framing stays consistent: this is not a publicity stunt, it is a working trial. Real operators want to use the airplane, and flying the route is how BETA learns what has to improve before the aircraft is useful and economic on an everyday basis. They are open about the conditions, noting that flying over open water in the forties means weather and strong wind are constant company, and that partnering with an airline is how you fold real operational pressure into development. The word they keep returning to is paradigm. In their telling, electric aviation is not a far-off promise but a near-term shift that an airline can actually plan its domestic network around. There are small tells of how routine they want this to feel, from the matter-of-fact radio call on departure to the framing of Air New Zealand as a domestic operator thinking about real passengers, not a marketing partner. The team is open that the airplane is not finished, and that flying it in real conditions is how the rough edges get found before anyone tries to sell tickets.

Bottom line: Range anxiety killed the easy version of electric aviation years ago, so the smart play is to stop fighting physics and choose the routes where short range is a feature rather than a flaw. A 20-minute hop over water that the ferry needs three hours to cover is exactly that route. The figure to watch is not top speed or even range, it is cost per seat, and a 15 dollar crossing that beats the ferry on fuel before it even leaves the dock is the sort of math that turns an airline trial into a real order. If this scales beyond a handful of flights, New Zealand will have handed the rest of the world a working playbook.
