The Drift Car and the Driverless Car

nexusEVnews Daily Brief  ·  Jul 21, 2026

Line up today's five stories and they fall into two camps that barely speak to each other. Two are about a person in a seat, grinning, doing something slightly irresponsible with a lot of electric torque. The other three are about making the car quieter, cheaper, and eventually invisible, until it is less a machine you drive and more a piece of infrastructure that runs in the background. The electric car is becoming a toy and a utility at the same time, and which one you see depends on where you look.

Start with the toys. Renault took its 1980 rally hatch and rebuilt it as a 555hp electric throwback, a limited-run, 555-horsepower drift machine with a hydraulic handbrake and no real purpose beyond delight. On the other side of the world, Jay Leno spent an afternoon sliding a Polestar 4 Arctic Circle, a rally build made to slide across ice and dirt while its engineer explains why instant torque is more fun than any turbo. Neither car is about range anxiety or total cost of ownership. They exist to prove an electric car can still make you laugh.

Then there is the other electric car, the one that disappears. China did not just build EVs, it built an electric society, where battery swaps take four minutes and the charging network is measured in one plug per ten cars. That same supply chain now feeds China's robotaxis, betting on the same playbook, the driverless taxis using cheap lidar to take the driver out of the loop entirely. And in Bavaria, where German startups want two harvests from one field, farmers are turning fields into power plants, letting the panels quietly do a second job while the crops grow underneath. None of these ask you to enjoy driving. They ask you not to think about it at all.

What to watch is which vision sets the defaults. Europe, on today's evidence, still owns the emotional register: the heritage revivals, the driver's cars, the clever use of scarce land. China owns scale and is now spending it on autonomy. The risk for the joy camp is that systemic bets, the swap stations, the robotaxis, the solar over farmland, are the ones that quietly decide what an ordinary car is by the end of the decade, while the drift machines stay a lovely, expensive footnote.

Bottom line: The fun cars win the internet and the systemic bets win the market. Renault and Polestar are building the EVs people will remember, but China and Europe's quiet infrastructure plays are building the ones everyone will actually use. If you care about driving, savor this moment. The cars that make you smile are getting rarer, and the ones that ask nothing of you are getting cheaper by the month.
