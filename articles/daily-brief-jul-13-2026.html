The Transition Is Being Won at the Cheap End

nexusEVnews Daily Brief  ·  Jul 13, 2026

Line up today's five stories by price and a split appears. On one side sit the cheap, the used, and the small: a wave of off-lease EVs about to make late-model electric cars a mainstream used buy, an $8,000 microcar built to undercut the Citroen Ami, and a pair of $40,000-ish crossovers fighting over the same family driveway. On the other side sit the moonshots: a maglev line that will not open until 2036, and an electric aircraft still chasing certification in 2028. The near-term wins in electrification are turning out to be small, cheap, and a little boring. The grand stuff keeps slipping right.

The clearest signal is on the used side. In MotorTrend's interview, the CEO of Plug Motors describes roughly a million EVs coming back off lease over three years, and independent industry data backs the shape of it. That wave, plus gas the hosts pegged near $4.50 a gallon, turns cars dealers dreaded a year ago into the affordable entry point the market has lacked. The used-EV flood is the story to watch. The microcar tells the same tale from the other end: Astraux's AL7 Pro is trying to win Europe not with range or luxury but by fixing the Ami's two worst limits, speed and air conditioning, at a similar price. A cheap city EV that might actually work. Even the crossover comparison is fundamentally about value, two sensible small EVs doing the same job for the same money. Volvo charm versus Subaru practicality.

Contrast that with the megaprojects. Japan's Chuo maglev finally cleared its last big hurdle, and it is a genuine milestone, but NHK's own reporting puts the opening a decade out and the cost near double the original estimate. A green light that still means 2036. Vertical Aerospace's electric aircraft is a marvel of engineering, and the batteries really have made it possible, yet the honest timeline is still 2028 at the earliest. The physics is ready; the paperwork is not. Both are worth doing. Neither changes how anyone gets around this year.

Over the next six months, the interesting action is at the bottom of the market. Watch used EV prices as the lease wave builds, and watch whether cheap city cars like the AL7 Pro actually clear Europe's homologation rules. That is where adoption is being decided, not on a test track in the Cotswolds or in a tunnel under the Southern Alps. The megaprojects will get their headlines when they open. The transition is being won in the meantime, quietly, by cars people can already afford.

Bottom line: The future of mobility keeps getting sold as maglev lines and air taxis. It is actually being won by a three-year-old Model 3 under $20,000 and an $8,000 box with air conditioning. Cheap and available beats fast and far, at least for now.
